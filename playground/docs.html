Field Guide: Sourdough at High Altitude

Above 5,000 feet, dough ferments faster, water boils cooler, and the crumb dries out before you have named it. This is a working method for a 70–75% hydration country loaf in thin air — written for a kitchen at 5,280 feet and tested up to 7,200.

Why altitude changes the loaf

Three things happen at once. Lower air pressure lets gas expand sooner, so a dough that looks sleepy at sea level can overproof on a kitchen counter in Denver. Water boils near 202°F at 5,280 feet, which means the crumb sets later and the crust can color before the center is done. The air is drier, so the surface skins over and tears instead of stretching.

You do not need a new religion. You need a slightly wetter dough, a cooler proof, and a bake that spends more time covered. The rest is timing with your eyes, not the clock on the oven.

Rule of thumb For every 1,500 feet above 3,000, expect fermentation to run 15–25% faster in a 75°F room. If your kitchen is 80°F in August, assume the high end.

The starter

A sluggish starter is the most common high-altitude failure, because the dough itself is already in a hurry. Feed a stiff-ish culture (about 80% hydration) twice in the 12 hours before mix if it has been living in the fridge. Peak should be domed, just starting to recede, and smell like ripe apple — not nail polish.

If you only keep a wet jar, that still works. Use it at a younger peak than you would at sea level. Once it doubles and the bubbles are fine rather than cavernous, mix. Waiting for a dramatic collapse is how you bake vinegar.

Formula

This is one loaf, about 900 grams raw dough. Baker’s percentages are against total flour. If you live above 6,500 feet, start at 75% water and be ready to hold back a tablespoon.

Ingredient Weight Baker’s %
Bread flour (12.5–13% protein) 400 g 80%
Whole wheat, freshly milled if you can 100 g 20%
Water, 80°F 360–375 g 72–75%
Ripe starter 80 g 16%
Fine salt 10 g 2%
Flour note High-altitude flour often behaves thirstier because it is drier in the bag. If the dough looks like batter after the first mix, you did not fail — wait 20 minutes. Autolyse is not optional up here.

Mix and fold

Step 1

Autolyse

Stir flour and most of the water until no dry pockets remain. Cover 30–40 minutes. Hold back 15 g of water to wash the salt and starter in later. The dough should look shaggy, not soup.

Step 2

Salt, starter, remaining water

Pinch in the starter, then the salt slurry. Squeeze and fold until the dough is one piece again. It will feel sticky. That is hydration doing its job, not a sign you should dump in more flour.

Step 3

Coil folds

Three or four sets of coil folds over 90 minutes, 20–25 minutes apart, in a lightly wet bowl. Stop when the dough holds a rounded shape for ten seconds after a fold. Extra folds after that tear gluten that is already organized.

Proof

Bulk at 74–76°F if you can. In a warm apartment, that may mean a cooler corner, not the counter by the stove. You are looking for a 40–50% rise and a dome that jiggles as one piece. A full doubling is usually too far at altitude.

Preshape, rest 20 minutes, then a tight final shape into a banneton dusted with rice flour. Cold proof 10–14 hours at 38–40°F. The dough should spring slowly when you press a floured knuckle, not collapse into the fingerprint.

If you skip the fridge A same-day final proof at 75°F is 45–70 minutes. Check at 40. Overproofed dough here does not look dramatically flat — it just opens in the oven like a sad hat.

Bake

Preheat a Dutch oven at 475°F for 45 minutes. Score with a shallow, confident cut — deep ears tear in dry air. Bake covered 22 minutes, then uncovered 16–20 minutes at 450°F until the crust is a hard mahogany and the loaf sounds hollow. Internal temperature should land around 208–210°F; the usual 205°F sea-level cue is often a touch underdone here.

Cool on a rack at least 90 minutes. Cutting early at altitude gives you a gummy slice and a speech you will regret giving.

Troubleshooting

Dense crumb, tight line under the crust. Underfermented or too cold a bake. Give bulk another 20 minutes, or keep the lid on 3 minutes longer.

Huge holes and a collapsed side. Overproof. Cut starter to 12%, or drop bulk temperature two degrees.

Pale crust, wet center. Water is boiling late. Uncover earlier and finish at 455°F. A baking steel under the pot helps the bottom catch up.

Skin tears during shape. Mist the bench, not the dough. A dry surface at 15% humidity is a paper bag.
